01 · PHYSICS · CHEMISTRY≈ 40 min

Bearings & friction

Once the cartridge is empty, the car coasts, and every bit of friction steals speed. Rolling resistance at the axles is roughly a constant force, μ·m·g, so a heavier car and dirtier bearings both lose more. Cleaning bearings with isopropyl alcohol (IPA) removes old grease and dust; ceramic balls are lighter and harder than steel, so they roll with less resistance.

F_roll = μ · m · g

Worked example

A 55 g car (0.055 kg), g = 9.81 m/s².

BearingsμForce
Steel, dusty0.0220.0119 N
Steel, cleaned0.0120.0065 N
Ceramic, cleaned0.0060.0032 N

Over 20 m, cleaning steel bearings saves about 0.0054 N × 20 m ≈ 0.11 J of energy.

Our car

ORB!TUS runs BOCA hybrid ceramic bearings on a 51 g car. With ceramic bearings (μ ≈ 0.006) the rolling force is 0.006 × 0.051 × 9.81 ≈ 0.0030 N, against about 0.0110 N with dusty steel bearings: roughly a quarter of the friction.

Spin-down test

Spin a wheel by hand and time how long it takes to stop. Repeat five times for each bearing and compare the averages. Longer spin = less friction. Change only one thing between tests.

02 · PHYSICS≈ 35 min

Newton’s third law

Forces always come in pairs: if A pushes B, B pushes A back with the same size force in the opposite direction. The key is that the two forces act on different objects, so they never cancel each other out.

At the launch

The car pushes the gas backwards out of the cartridge; the gas pushes the car forwards. That forward push on the car is the thrust.

At the stop

The car hits the catch and pushes on it; the catch pushes back on the car with an equal force, which stops it.

The common mistake: drawing both forces of a pair on the same object. Draw each arrow on the object it acts on.

Our car

ORB!TUS weighs 51 g, so the Earth pulls it down with 0.051 × 9.81 ≈ 0.50 N, and ORB!TUS pulls the Earth up with the same 0.50 N. The track pushing up on the car is a separate pair.

03 · MATHS · PHYSICS≈ 40 min

Drag maths

Air resistance grows with the square of speed: double the speed and the drag is four times bigger. That is why shape and frontal area matter so much at 20 m/s.

F_drag = ½ · ρ · v² · C_d · A

The letters

  • ρ: air density, about 1.20 kg/m³
  • v: speed in m/s
  • C_d: drag coefficient (shape)
  • A: frontal area in m² (13 cm² = 0.0013 m²)

Worked example

C_d = 0.6, A = 0.0013 m², v = 20 m/s:

½ × 1.20 × 400 × 0.6 × 0.0013 = 0.187 N

At 10 m/s it is only 0.047 N, a quarter as much.

Our car

ORB!TUS’s predicted 1.088 s means an average speed of 20 ÷ 1.088 ≈ 18.4 m/s. At that speed, a car with the example shape above (C_d 0.6, 13 cm²) would feel ½ × 1.20 × 18.4² × 0.6 × 0.0013 ≈ 0.16 N of drag. (The shape values are examples, not ORB!TUS measurements.)

05 · PHYSICS≈ 45 min

Launch: energy, momentum & impulse

The cartridge gives the car a short, strong push. The total push is the impulse: force multiplied by time, or the area under the force–time graph. Impulse equals the change in momentum.

Impulse = F × t = Δp = m × Δv

Worked example

Thrust starting at 6 N and fading with τ = 0.25 s gives an impulse of 6 × 0.25 = 1.5 N·s.

For a 55 g car with no losses: Δv = 1.5 ÷ 0.055 ≈ 27 m/s, and kinetic energy ½mv² ≈ 20.5 J.

Where the energy goes

Real cars finish far slower because drag and rolling resistance take energy away the whole run. Use the race bench above: it shows the energy lost to drag for your settings.

Our car

For ORB!TUS (51 g), the same 1.5 N·s with no losses gives 1.5 ÷ 0.051 ≈ 29.4 m/s and ½ × 0.051 × 29.4² ≈ 22 J. Its predicted 1.088 s run averages 18.4 m/s, so drag and friction take a large share of that energy over 20 m.

06 · CHEMISTRY≈ 40 min

The CO₂ cartridge: moles, gas & pressure

An 8 g cartridge holds liquid carbon dioxide under high pressure. When it is pierced, the liquid turns into gas and expands to hundreds of times its volume in a fraction of a second.

n = m ÷ M pV = nRT

Worked example

Molar mass of CO₂ = 44.0 g/mol, so n = 8 ÷ 44.0 = 0.18 mol.

At 20 °C (293 K) and normal pressure (101 325 Pa):

V = nRT ÷ p = 0.18 × 8.314 × 293 ÷ 101 325 ≈ 0.0044 m³

That is about 4.4 litres of gas from 8 grams.

Why temperature matters

Pressure rises with temperature (p ∝ T at a fixed volume). A cartridge left in a warm pit area holds a higher pressure than one kept cool, so keep them at the same temperature for fair tests.

Our car

The 8 g of CO₂ is about 16% of ORB!TUS’s 51 g, and it leaves the car during the launch, so the car is lighter for the rest of the run.

07 · MATHS · DATA≈ 40 min

Race data & graphs

One run proves nothing. Run several times, find the mean and the range, and look for results that don’t fit. Then decide if a change really helped.

Worked example: six runs (s)

1.211.181.241.191.621.20

Mean of all six = 1.27 s. The 1.62 s run is far from the others (maybe a bad launch), so check what happened. Without it: mean = 1.20 s, range = 1.24 − 1.18 = 0.06 s.

Distance–time graphs

Plot distance against time using split timings. The gradient (slope) is the speed: steep at the start while the cartridge pushes, then flattening as drag and friction slow the car.

Our car

Compare every run with the prediction. ORB!TUS was predicted at 1.088 s, so a run of, say, 1.12 s would be (1.12 − 1.088) ÷ 1.088 ≈ 2.9% slower than predicted: worth checking the launch or the bearings.

These are learning numbers and simplified models. Always check your own event’s current rules for cartridge, mass, dimensions and safety, and test changes with repeated runs.
